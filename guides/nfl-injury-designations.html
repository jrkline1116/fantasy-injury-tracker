NFL Injury Designations Explained: Questionable, Doubtful, Out and IR

By John Kline · Updated October 16, 2026

Every week, each NFL team files an official injury report, and by the end of the week every player on it gets a game status. Those one-word labels decide more fantasy matchups than almost anything else, yet they're easy to misread. "Questionable" doesn't mean what it sounds like, "doubtful" almost always means the opposite of hopeful, and the injured reserve list has rules that change how long a player is gone.

This guide walks through each designation, when it's released, and what it should mean for your lineup.

The three game statuses

Practice reports earlier in the week only list who practiced and how much. The game status comes with the final report of the week. For a Sunday game that's Friday afternoon; Monday night teams file theirs on Saturday, and Thursday teams file earlier in the week. There are only three statuses:

Questionable (Q)
The player is uncertain to play. This is the widest category by far. It covers everything from a starter nursing a sore ankle who will play every snap to a player who is genuinely 50/50. Most questionable players end up playing, which is exactly why the label alone isn't enough to bench someone.
Doubtful (D)
The player is unlikely to play. In practice, doubtful players very rarely suit up. Treat doubtful as out for lineup purposes unless there's specific late news saying otherwise.
Out (O)
The player will not play. No ambiguity: replace him.

A player with no designation on the final report is expected to play. The old "probable" label was dropped by the league in 2016, so if you see it on a site, the site is out of date.

Why questionable is the hard one

Because questionable covers such a broad range, the label is only the start of the story. Three things tell you much more:

  • The practice trend. A player who goes from did-not-participate on Wednesday to limited on Thursday to full on Friday is very likely to play. A player who never practiced all week and is still listed questionable is a real risk. Our practice report guide covers how to read the trend.
  • The injury itself. A hamstring or calf strain is riskier than a bruise, both for playing at all and for leaving the game early. A player can also be limited in role even when he's active.
  • Late reporting. Beat reporters often hear on Saturday or Sunday morning whether a questionable player is expected to go. That news usually lands before the official inactives list.

The single most reliable answer comes about 90 minutes before kickoff, when teams release their inactives. See the game-day inactives guide for how to use that window.

Changes after the final report

The final report isn't always final. Teams can add a player on Saturday, typically for an illness or something that flared up in the last practice, and they can downgrade a questionable player to doubtful or out before the game. They rarely upgrade. If a player you're starting is added to the report late in the week, that's worth paying attention to, because it usually means something changed.

Injured reserve (IR)

Injured reserve is a roster move, not a game status. A player placed on IR doesn't count against the team's active roster, and he has to miss a minimum number of games before he can come back. Under the current rules that minimum is four games, and each team can bring only a limited number of players back from IR over a season. When a team is ready to bring a player back, it designates him to return, which opens a practice window; he must be activated within that window or he stays on IR.

For fantasy, IR means "plan for at least a month, probably longer." It's also the clearest signal that a backup's role is about to grow, which is why IR news is worth acting on quickly on the waiver wire.

PUP, NFI and suspensions

PUP (physically unable to perform)
For football injuries carried over from the previous season or the offseason. A player on the reserve PUP list misses at least the first four games of the season.
NFI (non-football injury or illness)
The same idea for an injury or illness that didn't happen playing football. The time missed works much like PUP.
Suspended (SUS)
Not an injury, but it removes a player for a known number of games, so it belongs on any availability report.

These lists matter most in August and September, when drafters sometimes forget a player won't be available for the first month.

A simple lineup rule of thumb

  1. Out or doubtful: replace him now, don't wait.
  2. Questionable and practiced fully on Friday: usually safe to start; confirm with the inactives list.
  3. Questionable after limited or no practice: line up a backup who plays later in the day if you can, and decide at the inactives release.
  4. Questionable in a late game: keep an early-game replacement on your bench only if your league locks players at their own kickoff. Otherwise make sure your flex spot holds the later player so you can still swap.

Injury information on this site comes from public team reports. It isn't medical advice, and no one outside a team knows exactly how a player is feeling on game day.
